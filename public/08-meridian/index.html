Essay · 02

p. 16

02

On
Precision

The editors
1,412 characters · 2 min

Precision is not fussiness. Fussiness is what happens when a designer decorates doubt; precision is what happens when a designer removes it. The two are often confused, because both involve small movements — a rule nudged half a point, a numeral swapped for its tabular twin — but only one of them has a direction.

Josef Müller-Brockmann kept his grids not because he loved constraint but because he distrusted improvisation. A grid is a promise made in advance: this is where things will go, and therefore this is where the reader will find them. Every deviation spends attention the argument may need later. The Swiss did not banish expression; they moved it into proportion, where it could be checked.

Consider the humble rule — one hairline. Set it on the baseline and it disappears into the text's rhythm. Set it two points off and every paragraph downstream inherits the wobble. Precision compounds. So does its absence.

This is why precision is a moral position before it is a technique. It says: the reader's time is real, the message is the point, the designer is not. It refuses the small vanities — the arbitrary indent, the ornamental italic — not because they are ugly but because they are unearned.

The test is simple. Remove one element. If nothing collapses, it was decoration. If the page falls apart, it was structure. Precision is the practice of building pages that fail that test everywhere.
