Below you: 10,935 metres of open water, and almost none of it has ever been seen. The light gives up in the first two hundred. You won't.

— 200 metres. The last usable sunlight dissolves here. Everything below has been dark for four billion years.

Photosynthesis ends. From this point down, light is something animals make.

Atolla wyvillei

the alarm jelly

When seized, the Atolla spins a silent pinwheel of blue light visible thirty metres away — a burglar alarm meant to summon something large enough to eat whatever is eating it.

Melanocetus johnsonii

the humpback anglerfish

Her lure glows with a colony of captive bacteria. When a male finds her in the dark, he bites on and dissolves — skin to skin, then vein to vein. Two animals, one bloodstream, no way home.

3,000 m · midnight zone

03 / 05

Eurypharynx pelecanoides

the gulper eel

Mostly mouth, barely muscle. Its jaws unhinge around prey larger than itself, and its whip of a tail ends in a small pink light — bait, dangled a full body-length away from the teeth.

Grimpoteuthis

the dumbo octopus

The deepest-living octopus known, filmed nearly seven kilometres down. It rows through the abyss on ear-like fins and has given up its ink entirely — in a world without light, there is nothing left to blind.

— 8,000 metres. The hadal zone, named for the god of the underworld. Eight hundred atmospheres press on every centimetre of you.

Snailfish live here — translucent, soft-boned, unbothered. Nothing about the deep asks permission to be alive.

10,935 m · challenger deep

05 / 05

Bathyscaphe Trieste

23 January 1960 · Jacques Piccard & Don Walsh

Four hours and forty-seven minutes of falling. Twenty minutes on the bottom. Through the single porthole, Piccard swore he watched a flatfish glide calmly away — life, waiting at the deepest place on Earth, entirely unimpressed by the visit.

The deep sea is not empty. It is only unlit.
